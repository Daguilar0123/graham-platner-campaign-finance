What Can $11 Million Buy?

It just might have bought the derailment of a people-powered movement.

Between October 2025 and July 8, 2026 — the day Graham Platner announced he was withdrawing from the race — outside groups reported $11.4 million in independent expenditures about his candidacy to the Federal Election Commission. That money was not evenly split: $11.1 million of it was spent opposing him, against $323K supporting him. Nearly all of the opposing money came through a single super PAC, Pine Tree Results PAC, paying a single media vendor — and part of that PAC's funding traces to 501(c)(4) nonprofits that do not disclose their donors. This page lists every FEC-filed independent expenditure for or against Platner, each linked to the actual filing, along with the money flows that never appear in FEC data.

How to read this bar. Platner effectively ran two races back to back: first the Democratic primary, decided June 9, then the general-election matchup against Susan Collins — which ended for him when he announced his withdrawal on July 8. The bar is every outside dollar reported in the race, as one whole. The dark red half is opposition money spent before primary day ($5.75M). The lighter red half is opposition money spent in the four weeks after he won the primary ($5.33M). Notice those halves are nearly equal: winning the nomination didn't slow the spending against him — the pace held at roughly $1.3M a week from April through his withdrawal announcement. The green sliver on the far right is every dollar spent supporting him all year: $323,426, or about 2.8 cents of each outside dollar in the race.

Where the opposing money went

In plain terms: this was not a crowd of groups independently deciding to spend against one candidate. Of the $11.08M in opposition money, $10.72M — about 97 cents of every dollar — came from a single super PAC, Pine Tree Results PAC. And every one of that PAC's six purchases went to the same ad-buying firm, SRCP Media, in checks of roughly $1.5–2M apiece. The other three opposing committees spent about $354K combined. So the shorthand "outside groups spent $11 million against him" really means: one PAC wrote six large checks to one vendor, and a few small players rounded it out.

The money you can't trace — following it upstream

The spending is public.
The source isn't.

What "dark money" actually means here — no metaphors, just the rule. Everything in the table above is money a super PAC spent. The FEC requires every dollar of that to be reported, which is why this page can list it down to the cent, with receipts. And a super PAC must also name its donors. But here's the design of the system: when one of those donors is a 501(c)(4) "social welfare" nonprofit, the disclosure obligation ends at the nonprofit's name — because that kind of nonprofit is allowed to raise unlimited money without ever naming its donors. Walk the trail yourself:
Step 1 · On the public record
Pine Tree Results PAC paid $10.7M to an ad firm to oppose a candidate. Filed with the FEC. You can read every receipt above.
→
Step 2 · On the public record
A nonprofit called Stronger America, Inc. put $3,000,000 into that PAC on Dec 29, 2025 — it's right there in the PAC's own FEC donor filings. A name and a number — so far, so visible.
→
Step 3 · The record ends here
?
Who gave the nonprofit the $3,000,000? No filing answers that. None exists. Not hidden in fine print — legally never written down for the public. This is the step the word "dark" refers to.

This isn't a loophole someone found last year; it's how 501(c)(4) disclosure law works in every federal race. What makes it concrete here: for the largest spender in this race, the PAC's own FEC donor filings show both the visible donors and the two nonprofits where the trail stops — with donor-network reporting from The Nation and the Bangor Daily News filling in who runs those nonprofits. Why the news reports even bigger numbers

The FEC table above counts only formally itemized "independent expenditures." News outlets also count things the FEC table doesn't — airtime reserved in advance, ad-tracker estimates, and ads that don't legally count as independent expenditures. By those broader counts:

  • GOP-aligned committees: ~$19M in broadcast + digital across the race; Democratic-aligned groups: ~$6.4M. The Hill ↗
  • Pine Tree Results' attack-ad pace was reported at roughly $1.5M a week. The Nation ↗

How this page was built

We asked the FEC's public database for every independent expenditure filed about this candidate and got 30 line items back, then opened the actual scanned filings to verify each one. (Technical note for anyone reproducing this: query api.open.fec.gov/v1/schedules/schedule_e/?candidate_id=S6ME00373&per_page=100 — searching by the name "platner" instead of the candidate ID returns unrelated records.) Adding all 30 raw line items gives $15,399,598.89; after removing the double-reported filings described above, the honest total is $11.4M. Press figures run higher than FEC figures because they estimate more broadly — that's a difference in what's being counted, not a contradiction.
